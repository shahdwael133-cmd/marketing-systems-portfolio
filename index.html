I turned a one-person marketing function into software.

I joined a B2B on-demand manufacturing platform as its only marketer. No analysts, no designers, no engineers, no tooling budget.

The title said marketing. The real problem was infrastructure: fragmented data, finite hours, competitors nobody was watching, and buyers who hesitate at the exact moment they're asked to upload a file.

So I ran growth the way a product team runs a product. Twelve weeks later, 54 systems are live or in final development, 7 of them on cron in the cloud. I come from business, not computer science. Every line was written with AI coding agents as my pair programmer, and every architectural decision was mine.

02 · the problem

Six constraints, one person

Custom metal parts are a high-stakes purchase. An engineer arrives with a drawing, a deadline and a supplier base they've learned not to trust. The platform exists to collapse that uncertainty into a quote and a delivery date.

Its growth had the same problem one level up. Here is what I was working against, and what I built against each one.

A 3D CAD viewer that never uploads your file

Roughly 5,400 people in the US search for a STEP file viewer every month. Nearly all of them are engineers. That's the exact customer, already holding a part that needs to be made.

Insight

Engineers guard their geometry. Asking for an upload before any trust exists puts friction at the worst possible moment. So the viewer had to run entirely in the browser, and the privacy guarantee became the pitch.

A free utility with a quote button beside it does acquisition by itself. The tool is the funnel.

The design choice that matters most

Language models narrate well and count badly. So 2,765 lines of deterministic code own every number, and the model only writes the paragraph around them. Same inputs, same outputs, every run. If the arithmetic is ever wrong, it's a bug I can find, not a hallucination I can't reproduce.

DistributionA login-gated linkA public, read-only web endpointA test on 10 Sep showed logged-out stakeholders couldn't open the gated version.

The failure that made it better

v1 ran on my laptop through Windows Task Scheduler and failed 3 times unattended. Patching it would have meant babysitting it. Status

Replacing opinions with an experiment

A website rebuild was coming, and every decision was being argued on taste. Nobody agreed on what a qualified lead was, so nobody could say whether anything worked.

What I put in place, in order

Clean telemetry. I cut the event taxonomy to 3 conversion events (quote_click, form_submit, a call booking), each counted once, and extended data retention to 14 months.

A shared definition. Every inbound lead gets an A/B/C fit grade, so sales and marketing are counting the same thing.

A baseline before any change. Three weeks of untouched data, then changes released inside a fixed window.

Power analysis up front. I measured the week-to-week noise before the test began, so I know the smallest effect that counts as real. A test that can't detect the effect you care about tells you nothing when it comes back flat.

11 · principles

Rules I don't break

end_to_end

A system runs from data in to result delivered. No manual step hiding in the middle.

fail_closed

When inputs are wrong, the system stops and tells me. It never guesses.

dry_run_first

Every automation passes a full test run and ships with a written runbook.

acceptance_criteria

Pass/fail rules get written before the build. The output is scored against them.

ground_truth

Real data and verified facts only. No invented numbers, no synthetic people in content.

cost_ceiling

Free tiers first. Anything paid is tested at the smallest scale before full volume.
